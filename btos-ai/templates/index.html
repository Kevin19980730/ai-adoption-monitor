      Three different questions

      Firms (Census BTOS): the share of employer businesses that used any AI in a business function in the last two weeks. Each firm counts once, so the many small firms dominate.

      Workers (Real-Time Population Survey): the share of employed adults who use generative AI for their job.

      Consumers: company-stated weekly or monthly users of ChatGPT, Gemini and Meta AI.

      ! BTOS changed its AI question

      Until Oct 2025 the question asked about AI “in producing goods or services”. Since Dec 2025 it asks about AI “in any of its business functions”. That jump is the new wording, not real adoption. Charts show the old series dashed and never join the two. There is no data for 6 Oct–16 Nov 2025 (government shutdown).

      Top models by tokens, latest week

      Four tests of the “token-economics equilibrium”

      The thesis (fin, AI Semiconductor Endgame IV, Oct 2026): token prices keep falling, serving costs fall just as fast, so gross margins hold and labs keep buying GPUs. It holds only while usage grows faster than prices fall. Open source takes a large share of tokens but a small share of spend, because cheap tokens and premium tokens are separate markets. Each panel below tests one link. The thesis is in trouble if the elasticity stays below 1 for quarters, open-weights spend share closes on its token share, or margins fall on the same chip.

      1 · Is usage outrunning price cuts?

      Elasticity = % change in tokens ÷ % fall in price over the window. Above 1, spend grows as prices fall (Jevons); at 1 spend is flat; below 1 price cuts shrink revenue. Shown only when the price fell at least 5% over the window.

      2 · Open weights: share of tokens vs share of spend

      OpenRouter, every model, trailing 7 days. Open weights = the model has a Hugging Face release. Spend = tokens × that week’s list price; “cache-adjusted” bills 80% of input at the model’s cached-input price where it has one (OpenRouter does not publish cached-token counts). Free variants count as tokens with zero spend. History before 21 Oct 2026 comes from Wayback Machine captures of the same endpoints.

      OpenRouter skews to cheap open models (developers routing for price), so its levels are far above fin’s market-wide estimate of ~20% of tokens and 5–10% of revenue. The signal is the gap: if spend share starts closing on token share, open models are winning premium work, not just volume.

      3 · Open-model inference margin per GPU

      InferenceX (SemiAnalysis) measured throughput, as of each month-end, for 8,192-in / 1,024-out requests. Best config per chip that holds the service tier at P90. Revenue at the median provider list price on OpenRouter; cost = InferenceX owned TCO per GPU-hour. Gross margin = 1 − TCO ÷ (revenue × utilisation).

      What this measures

      The Ramp AI Index is the share of US businesses on Ramp’s corporate cards and bill pay that paid for an AI model, platform or tool in the month. It counts paid subscriptions and API bills. Free tools and AI that is bundled into other software are not counted. Ramp’s customers lean towards tech-forward, venture-backed firms, so the level runs well above the Census BTOS share. Both are shown on the first chart; read the gap as a measure of how far ahead Ramp’s customers are. These figures come from the same Ramp page as the Business tokens tab.

      Checked against the first-hand sources behind the footnote

      Each panel rebuilt from primary data and compared point by point with the PDF’s vector values. Railroads: the NBER tables the footnote names. O&G and telecoms (footnote: “BEA”): BEA Fixed Assets investment by industry, all asset types, over BEA GDP. Hyperscalers: SEC filings of the five companies over BEA GDP.

      How BofA dates the points: every post-1970 point is drawn three quarters before the end of its four-quarter window (half a year before the start of its final quarter). The values and the periods they belong to are confirmed by the filings; only the horizontal placement is off, and it is the same for every post-1970 cycle, so levels, peaks and cycle-to-cycle comparisons are unaffected. The hyperscaler point drawn at mid-2021 is capex for the four quarters to Q1 2022, and the last point (drawn mid-2025) is the four quarters to Q1 2026. Annual year Y is drawn from mid-year Y−1 to Q1 of Y for the same reason. The dashed line below continues the first-hand hyperscaler series to the latest quarter all five have filed.

      Hyperscaler capex by company, each calendar quarter — plus CoreWeave, Nebius and SpaceX AI

      Purchases of property & equipment from each company’s 10-Q / 10-K (SEC XBRL), $bn per calendar quarter. Quarters come from year-to-date cash flows (Q4 = full year minus nine months). Oracle’s fiscal quarters end Feb / May / Aug / Nov, so it is calendarised: each calendar quarter = ⅔ × the Oracle quarter ending in its second month + ⅓ × the next one. CoreWeave: purchases of property & equipment from its filings (quarterly from 2024). Nebius: a foreign private issuer, so full years come from its 20-F and quarters from its 6-K interim statements / earnings releases (purchases of property, equipment and intangible assets; 9M 2024 is one period). SpaceX AI: capital expenditures of SpaceX’s AI segment (xAI; renamed “Super Intelligence” in Oct 2026) from its IPO prospectus, 10-Q segment note and earnings releases (8-K, until the 10-Q lands). The Q3 2026 release gave Q3 2025 alone, so Q4 2025 = full year minus nine months. BofA’s comparison above keeps its own five names. Refreshed by the weekly job as new filings land.

      Oracle fiscal quarters as filed

      Contracted backlog (RPO) vs capex

      Remaining performance obligations = revenue already contracted but not yet recognised, at each quarter end, from 10-Q / 10-K filings. Capex = purchases of property & equipment over the trailing four quarters (same series as above). Microsoft, Alphabet, Oracle: XBRL tag RevenueRemainingPerformanceObligation, company-wide. Amazon: no longer tagged after Q2 2020, so the figure is read from the revenue-note sentence in each filing (“commitments not yet recognized were approximately $X billion”, primarily AWS). Meta discloses no RPO, so it is left out of both lines. CoreWeave: same XBRL tag (from Q1 2025). Nebius: tag in its 20-F; interim quarters from its 6-K interim statements (from Dec 2025). SpaceX is left out: its backlog is company-wide only (no AI-segment split). Oracle’s quarter ends (Aug / Nov / Feb / May) are placed in the calendar quarter they fall in. Big step-ups line up with multi-year AI compute contracts.

      Construction in progress vs capex

      Construction in progress (CIP) = capex already spent on data centres, servers and other assets not yet placed in service, so not yet depreciating. It is read from the property & equipment note of each 10-Q / 10-K (XBRL, PP&E-by-type axis), at each quarter end. Capex = purchases of property & equipment over the trailing four quarters (same series as above). Gross PP&E = net PP&E + accumulated depreciation, so CIP is always inside it. Alphabet: called “assets not yet in service” from 2025 and shown below accumulated depreciation; same thing. Meta, CoreWeave: every quarter. Amazon: 10-K only (December points). Oracle: 10-K only (May year ends) until Aug 2026, when its 10-Q started giving it. Nebius: “assets not yet in use” in its 6-K interim statements, incl. equipment prepayments. SpaceX is left out: its CIP is company-wide only (no AI-segment split). Microsoft discloses no CIP; its nearest pipeline measure is leases signed but not yet commenced (lease note, ~$329bn at Jun 2026). A rising CIP ÷ capex means spend is running ahead of capacity going live, so depreciation is still to come.

      Built from company filings and BEA GDP only. How these numbers compare with BofA’s exhibit, and the earlier capex cycles, is on the Capex cycles tab (Macro).

      US 10-year yield: real yield and term premium

      Percent, daily. Real yield = 10-year TIPS yield (FRED DFII10, from 2003): the inflation-adjusted discount rate for long-duration assets such as AI equities. Term premium = the extra yield investors demand to hold a 10-year bond rather than roll short bills, from the Fed Board’s Kim–Wright model (FRED THREEFYTP10, from 1990; published about a week late). Nominal 10-year yield (DGS10) and breakeven inflation (nominal − real) for context. Shaded = NBER recessions.

      US high-yield option-adjusted spreads, BB vs CCC & lower

      ICE BofA index OAS over Treasuries, daily close, percent. FRED BAMLH0A1HYBB and BAMLH0A3HYC. FRED only serves the last 3 years (ICE licence), so 1996–2023 comes from archived copies of FRED’s full-history files; the two match exactly where they overlap. Shaded = NBER recessions.

      Quality decompression: CCC minus BB

      Percentage points. A widening gap while BB stays tight means stress is building in the weakest credits first. Dashed line = median since 1996.

      CCC ÷ BB ratio

      Times. Removes the common market level, so it isolates how much extra the weakest borrowers pay. Dashed line = median since 1996.

      Latest readings and history

      Changes in basis points (bp). Real yield history starts 2003, term premium 1990, nominal 1962, spreads 1996. Percentile = share of trading days at or below today’s level; low = tight / complacent, high = stressed.
